# Your Rights Against Debt Collectors (FDCPA), Explained

Getting calls at 6 a.m., at your job, and from someone threatening you with arrest over an old bill is frightening — and it's often illegal. A federal law called the **Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)** puts strict limits on how debt collectors can behave, and knowing those limits turns a scary situation into a manageable one. Here's what the law actually protects, in plain English.

## Who the FDCPA covers

An important nuance up front: the FDCPA generally applies to **third-party debt collectors** — companies collecting debts owed to someone else, including collection agencies and debt buyers. It typically does *not* cover the original creditor collecting its own debt (though many states have their own laws that reach original creditors too, and those vary by state).

It also applies to **personal, family, or household debts** — things like credit cards, medical bills, and personal loans — not most business debts. If a collection agency is calling about a consumer debt, you're almost certainly in FDCPA territory.

## Banned harassment and call times

The FDCPA draws clear lines around how and when collectors can contact you. A collector generally **cannot**:

- Call you at **unusual or inconvenient times** — as a default, before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. in your local time.
- Contact you at **work** if they know your employer prohibits such calls.
- **Harass or abuse** you — repeated calls meant to annoy, obscene language, or threats of violence are all off-limits.
- **Lie** about who they are, how much you owe, or the consequences — including impersonating a lawyer, a government agency, or law enforcement.
- **Threaten arrest** for an ordinary unpaid consumer debt. You generally cannot be jailed for owing money on a bill, and a collector saying otherwise is likely breaking the law.

## Your right to a debt verification letter

One of the most useful tools in the FDCPA is **debt validation**. Within five days of first contacting you, a collector generally must send a written notice stating the amount owed and the creditor. If you **dispute the debt in writing within 30 days**, the collector must stop collection efforts until it sends you verification of the debt.

This matters because debts get bought, sold, and mixed up constantly. Requesting verification is a reasonable, powerful first step — especially if you're not sure the debt is yours, the amount is wrong, or it may be too old. Always make the request **in writing** and keep a copy.

## Sending a cease-contact request

You also have the right to tell a collector, in writing, to **stop contacting you**. Once a collector receives that letter, it generally must stop — with narrow exceptions, such as notifying you that a specific action (like a lawsuit) is being taken.

A few cautions worth understanding:

- A cease-contact letter stops the *calls*, but it does **not** erase the debt. The collector can still pursue other legal remedies.
- Send it by a method that gives you proof of delivery, and keep your copy.
- Cutting off contact can sometimes prompt a collector to escalate, so weigh the decision based on your situation.

## Illegal threats and tactics to watch for

Beyond the basics, collectors are barred from a range of deceptive and unfair tactics: falsely claiming you've committed a crime, threatening to take actions they can't legally take, adding fees or interest not authorized by the agreement or law, and discussing your debt with third parties like your neighbors or coworkers (they can contact others only in limited ways, generally just to find your location).

## Suing a collector that breaks the rules

The FDCPA has teeth. If a collector violates it, you may be able to sue — generally within one year of the violation — and recover your actual damages, statutory damages up to a set amount, plus attorney's fees and costs. You can also report violations to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) and the Federal Trade Commission (FTC). Keeping a log of calls — dates, times, what was said — makes any complaint or claim far stronger.

## The takeaway

You have more power in a collections call than it feels like in the moment. The FDCPA bans harassment, threats, lies, and calls at unreasonable hours; it gives you the right to demand written verification of the debt and to require a collector to stop contacting you. Stay calm, get everything in writing, keep records, and remember that a collector breaking these rules can be held accountable.
