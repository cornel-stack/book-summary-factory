# Getting Your Security Deposit Back: A Plain-English Guide

Few things feel as quietly unfair as handing over a security deposit, keeping the place clean for a year, and then watching most of it vanish into vague "repairs." The good news: **getting your security deposit back** is far more rule-bound than most tenants realize. Landlords can't simply keep your money because they feel like it, and the law in most US states gives you specific protections. Here's how the system actually works, in plain English.

## Damage vs. normal wear and tear

The single biggest myth is that a landlord can deduct for a unit looking "used." They generally can't. The law almost everywhere draws a line between **damage** and **normal wear and tear**.

- **Normal wear and tear** is the ordinary aging that happens just from living somewhere: faded paint, lightly worn carpet, small nail holes from hanging pictures, minor scuffs on the wall. You don't pay for these. They're the cost of the landlord doing business.
- **Damage** is harm beyond ordinary use: a large hole in the wall, a pet-stained carpet, a cracked window, or filth that goes well past needing a routine cleaning.

A helpful rule of thumb: if it happened because time passed, it's probably wear and tear. If it happened because someone did something to the unit, it may be damage. The exact line can vary, but the principle holds in most US states — you're responsible for harm, not for the simple fact that you lived there.

## The itemized deduction statement

When a landlord keeps any part of your deposit, most US states require them to give you an **itemized statement** — a written, line-by-line list of what was deducted and why, usually with receipts or estimates for the work. A lump-sum "kept for damages, $600" with no breakdown is often not good enough, and in many states it weakens the landlord's position if the dispute ever reaches a judge.

If you get a vague deduction, you're entitled to ask for the itemization in writing. Keep that request, and keep their response.

## Deadlines for returning your money

Landlords don't get to sit on your deposit indefinitely. Most US states set a **deadline** for returning the deposit (and any itemized statement) after you move out — commonly somewhere in the range of 14 to 30 days, though the exact number varies by state and you should confirm your own. Miss that window, and the landlord may lose the right to keep anything, or even owe you a penalty (more on that below).

Because the deadline varies, the practical move is to look up your state's specific rule before you move out, so you know exactly when to expect your money and when a landlord has crossed the line.

## Your strongest evidence: photos

If a dispute happens, it usually comes down to one question: what did the unit look like? That's why documentation is your best friend.

- **At move-in,** photograph or video every room — floors, walls, appliances, fixtures — and note any pre-existing issues in writing. Share them with the landlord so there's a timestamped record.
- **At move-out,** do the same thing again, after you've cleaned. Date-stamped before-and-after photos are remarkably persuasive, because they turn a "he said, she said" argument into visible proof.

A tenant with clear move-in and move-out photos is in a dramatically stronger position than one relying on memory.

## What to do when a landlord won't pay

If the deadline passes or you get unfair deductions, you have options that escalate in formality:

1. **Send a written demand.** A calm, dated letter (keep a copy) citing your state's deposit law and deadline often resolves things on its own. Attach your photos and the itemized statement if you have one.
2. **Reference the penalty.** In many US states, a landlord who withholds a deposit in **bad faith** — ignoring the rules or keeping money they know they shouldn't — can be liable for extra damages, sometimes two or three times the amount wrongly withheld. Landlords who know this often reconsider.
3. **Use small claims court.** Deposit disputes are a classic small claims case: the amounts fit the court's limits, you usually don't need a lawyer, and your photo evidence does most of the work. (We'll cover small claims court in detail later this week.)

## The takeaway

Your security deposit is your money, held in trust — not a parting gift to your landlord. Know the difference between damage and wear and tear, insist on an itemized statement, watch your state's return deadline, and let your move-in and move-out photos tell the story.
