# How Small Claims Court Actually Works

If someone owes you a few thousand dollars — an unreturned deposit, a contractor who vanished, a friend who never paid you back — you might assume you need an expensive lawyer to do anything about it. You usually don't. **Small claims court** was built for exactly these everyday money disputes: it's faster, cheaper, and far less intimidating than the courtroom dramas on TV. Here's how it really works, in plain English.

## What small claims can and can't handle

Small claims court is designed for relatively simple disputes about **money**. Typical cases include unpaid debts, security deposit fights, minor property damage, and claims against a contractor or seller.

What it generally *can't* do is order someone to do something non-monetary — you usually can't use it to force a person to return property, stop a behavior, or undo a contract. Family-law matters like divorce and custody, and most complex cases, belong in other courts. If your dispute is essentially "this person owes me a specific amount of money," it's probably a good fit.

## Dollar limits that vary by state

Every small claims court has a **maximum dollar amount** it will hear, and this is one of the areas that **varies by state** — often somewhere in the range of a few thousand dollars up to around $25,000, depending on where you are. Check your own state's limit before filing.

If your claim is larger than the limit, you generally have two choices: file in a regular civil court instead, or voluntarily **waive the excess** and sue for the maximum your small claims court allows. Waiving can be worth it to keep the case simple, but you give up the right to collect the difference, so weigh it carefully.

## Filing and serving the other party

The process usually starts the same way everywhere:

1. **File a claim** with the right court — typically where the defendant lives or where the dispute happened — and pay a modest filing fee.
2. **Serve the other party.** The person you're suing (the defendant) must be formally notified, following your court's specific rules — often by certified mail or a process server. **Service of process matters:** if it isn't done correctly, your case can be delayed or dismissed.

Pay attention to deadlines, including your state's **statute of limitations**, which sets how long after the dispute you're allowed to file.

## Preparing your evidence and your story

Small claims judges are busy and practical. Your job is to make your case clear, organized, and easy to follow:

- **Bring documents.** Contracts, receipts, photos, text messages, emails, and a written timeline. Organize copies for yourself, the judge, and the other party.
- **Tell a simple story.** What was agreed, what went wrong, what you're owed, and why. No legal jargon needed — plain facts win.
- **Bring witnesses** if they genuinely help, or their written statements where your court allows.

Preparation is the single biggest predictor of how these cases go.

## Your day in court

Hearings are usually short and informal. You'll explain your side, the other party explains theirs, and the judge asks questions and decides — often quickly. In **many states, lawyers aren't even allowed** in small claims court, which is part of the point: it's meant to be navigable by ordinary people. (Whether lawyers are permitted varies by state.)

Dress neatly, show up early, stay calm and respectful, and answer the judge's questions directly. If the other side doesn't show up, you may win by **default** — but you'll still generally need to prove your basic case.

## Collecting on a judgment you win

Here's the part that surprises people most: **winning and getting paid are two different things.** A judgment is a court's official statement that you're owed money — it is *not* a check. If the other party doesn't pay voluntarily, you may have to take further steps to collect, such as wage garnishment or a bank levy, which have their own rules and often **vary by state**.

Collection is frequently the harder half of the whole process, so before you sue, it's worth asking a practical question: if I win, does this person actually have money or wages I could realistically collect from?

## The takeaway

Small claims court exists so that everyday people can resolve everyday money disputes without a lawyer or a fortune in fees. Know your state's dollar limit, serve the other party correctly, bring organized evidence, tell a clear story, and go in understanding that collecting may take extra effort after you win.
